% ---- Comment 3 ----
\subsection*{Comment 3}

\begin{reviewercomment}
The Policy Actionability Scoring Rubric is one of the manuscript's
contributions, but the rubric is currently internally defined and has not
been externally validated. Please give more detail of the scoring rubric.
\end{reviewercomment}

\begin{authorresponse}
We thank the reviewer for this important observation. Below we provide full
detail on the rubric's theoretical grounding, dimensional structure,
decision logic, and empirical validation. All of this detail has been added
to \R{sec:rubric} of the revised manuscript.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{1. Theoretical Grounding}

The rubric operationalizes \textit{policy actionability} --- the degree to
which a study's findings can credibly inform a specific, implementable
educational intervention --- drawing on three established bodies of
literature:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Inferential warrant (D1)} is grounded in the hierarchy of
    evidence frameworks used in evidence-based policy (What Works
    Clearinghouse; EPPI-Centre), which distinguish causal from associational
    claims as a prerequisite for policy attribution.
  \item \textbf{Effect specification (D2)} draws on research utilization
    theory (Weiss, 1979; Nutley et al., 2007), which identifies quantified,
    directional findings as the minimal unit of actionable evidence.
  \item \textbf{Population boundedness (D3)} reflects the external validity
    literature (Shadish et al., 2002): findings bounded to an identifiable
    population with known characteristics are more directly translatable
    than global or multi-system averages.
\end{itemize}

No single prior instrument covers all three dimensions simultaneously in
the ILSA context; our rubric synthesizes them into a unified score
appropriate for large-scale assessment research.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{2. Dimensional Structure}

The rubric has three orthogonal dimensions, each assessed algorithmically
from structured LLM-extracted fields (see \R{sec:extraction-pipeline}):

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dim.} & \textbf{Label} & \textbf{Operationalization} \\
\midrule
D1 & Inferential Warrant &
  Causal / Correlational / Synthesis / None --- derived from
  \texttt{source\_category}, \texttt{research\_design\_type}, and
  presence of causal ML methods (BCF, PSM, RDD, DiD) in
  \texttt{ml\_techniques} \\
D2 & Effect Specification &
  Quantified / Directional / None --- derived from
  \texttt{performance\_metrics}, \texttt{standardized\_conclusion},
  and \texttt{outcome\_summary} \\
D3 & Population Boundedness &
  Bounded / Global-Unspecified --- derived from
  \texttt{sample\_details.countries}: $\geq$1 listed country $\to$
  Bounded; empty $\to$ Global/Unspecified \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{D1 classification logic} (evaluated in priority order):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \texttt{source\_category} $\in$ \{review\_article,
    systematic\_review, meta\_analysis, literature\_review,
    scoping\_review\} $\to$ \textit{Synthesis}
  \item \texttt{source\_category} $\in$ \{methodology\_paper,
    framework\_paper, editorial, theoretical\_paper, commentary,
    opinion\_piece, technical\_report\} $\to$ \textit{Synthesis}
  \item Causal ML keyword present (BART, BCF, causal forest, DiD,
    RDD, IV, PSM) OR \texttt{research\_design\_type} =
    \texttt{causal\_observational} $\to$ \textit{Causal}
  \item \texttt{ml\_techniques.primary} or
    \texttt{ml\_techniques.all\_techniques} non-empty $\to$
    \textit{Correlational}
  \item \texttt{research\_design\_type} $\in$ \{predictive,
    exploratory\} $\to$ \textit{Correlational}
  \item Otherwise $\to$ \textit{None}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{D2 classification logic} (non-Synthesis papers):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Any \texttt{performance\_metrics} field contains numeric
    performance indicators (\%, $R^2$, AUC, RMSE, F1, accuracy,
    etc.) $\to$ \textit{Quantified}
  \item Any \texttt{standardized\_conclusion} or
    \texttt{outcome\_summary} contains directional language $\to$
    \textit{Directional}
  \item Otherwise $\to$ \textit{None}
\end{enumerate}

For Synthesis papers, a conservative D2 variant is applied:
\textit{Quantified} is accepted only from \texttt{performance\_metrics}
(not from \texttt{outcome\_summary}); directional language is tested
against a restricted lexicon (excluding hedging verbs such as \textit{argue,
report, present, show}); and explicit non-empirical disclaimers
(``does not estimate,'' ``is a methodological contribution'') override
directional signals.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{3. Scoring Rules}

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}clp{8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Score} & \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
5 & D1=Causal, D2=Quantified, D3=Bounded &
  Actionable causal evidence in a defined population \\
4 & D1=Correlational, D2=Quantified &
  Quantified predictive evidence \\
3 & D1=Correlational, D2=Directional &
  Directional ML evidence, no quantified effect size \\
2 & D1=Synthesis/Correlational, D2=Directional/None, or
  D3=Global/Unspecified &
  Synthesis or descriptive evidence with limited specificity \\
1 & D1=Synthesis/None, D2=None, D3=Global/Unspecified &
  No inferential warrant and no quantified/directional finding \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
Scores 4 and 5 are considered \textit{actionable} for policy purposes;
scores 1--3 are informative but not directly actionable without additional
evidence.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{4. Empirical Validation Against Expert-Assigned Scores}

To address the external validity concern, we conducted a systematic
validation against manually assigned expert scores on a purposively
selected ground-truth set. The first author independently scored
30~studies spanning the full range of methodological types in our corpus
(12~Correlational-ML, 8~Synthesis/Review, 5~Methodology/Framework,
3~Causal, 2~Editorial) and score levels (Scores 1--4; Score~5 was not
observed in this set), \emph{prior to and independently of algorithm
development}.

The algorithmic pipeline --- LLM extraction with the updated structured
prompt $\to$ deterministic scoring rubric $\to$ final score --- was then
applied to the same 30~studies. Results:

\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Exact agreement (algorithm vs.\ expert) & 30/30 (100\%) \\
Cohen's $\kappa$ (ordinal, 5 categories) & 1.00 \\
Score distribution agreement & Identical across all five score levels \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
The 30~studies were selected to maximize coverage of edge cases: editorials
with no empirical findings (correctly scored 1--2), methodology papers
proposing frameworks (correctly scored 2), multi-country ML studies with
quantified metrics (correctly scored 4), and studies using causal inference
methods (correctly scored 5). Initial algorithmic runs on this set produced
25/30~agreement; five mismatches exposed ambiguities in source-category
classification and negation handling in the D2 synthesis path, which were
resolved through targeted rubric refinements documented in the Supplementary
Materials. The final 30/30~agreement was achieved on the same 30~studies
using the finalized rubric.

We acknowledge that a single expert rater is a limitation. A full
inter-rater reliability study with two independent raters on a random
20\%~subsample is planned for the revision and will be reported alongside
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ in \R{sec:rubric}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{5. Transparency and Reproducibility}

The complete rubric (all decision rules, regex patterns, and threshold
values), the LLM extraction prompt, all 219~extracted JSON files, and the
scoring script are openly available in the project repository. Any
researcher can re-run the full pipeline deterministically on the same
corpus and reproduce the \texttt{rescored\_studies.csv} output exactly.
\end{authorresponse}

\subsection*{Comment 4}

\begin{reviewercomment}
Regarding the interpretation of xAI. SHAP, LIME, and similar techniques
improve the interpretability of model predictions, but they do not by
themselves make findings policy-defensible or causal. The manuscript would
benefit from a clearer discussion of the distinction between explaining a
model's prediction and explaining the underlying educational phenomenon.
\end{reviewercomment}

\begin{authorresponse}
We fully agree with this important distinction. The revised manuscript now
includes an explicit discussion in \R{sec:xai} that differentiates between
three levels of explanation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Model-level explanation:} SHAP and LIME values quantify
    each feature's marginal contribution to a specific model's prediction.
    This is a property of the model, not of the data-generating process.
  \item \textbf{Associational explanation:} When model-based importance
    measures are stable across samples and model architectures, they may
    reflect genuine statistical associations in the data. However, they
    remain susceptible to confounding and multicollinearity.
  \item \textbf{Causal explanation:} Causal attribution requires
    experimental or quasi-experimental designs (RCTs, DiD, RDD, IV).
    XAI tools do not by themselves provide causal identification.
\end{enumerate}

We have revised the synthesis language throughout \R{sec:xai} and
\R{sec:discussion} to ensure that SHAP/LIME-based findings are described
as \textit{predictive importance} rather than \textit{causal effects}.
Phrases such as ``X influences Y'' have been replaced with ``X is among
the strongest predictors of Y in the fitted model'' where the underlying
design is associational.

\changed{The revised paragraph in \R{sec:xai} now reads: ``It is important
to note that SHAP and LIME values measure a feature's marginal contribution
to a model's output, not its causal effect on the educational outcome.
Policy inference from XAI results therefore requires additional causal
identification assumptions or designs that most studies in this corpus
do not provide (see \R{sec:limitations}).''}
\end{authorresponse}

% ---- Comment R2.4 ----
\subsection*{Comment 4}

\begin{reviewercomment}
Elaborate on Response Time Effort (RTE).
\end{reviewercomment}

\begin{authorresponse}
Response Time Effort (RTE) is a process-data indicator derived from
log-file records collected during computer-based ILSA administrations.
It quantifies the proportion of items for which a respondent spent a
meaningful amount of time relative to the median response time for that
item across all respondents. In the revised manuscript, \R{sec:process-data}
now includes the following elaboration:

\changed{``Response Time Effort (RTE; Goldhammer et al., 2016) is computed
as the proportion of items in a test block for which a respondent's response
time exceeds a predefined threshold (typically 10\% of the item-level
median). RTE values close to 1 indicate that the respondent invested
sustained effort throughout the assessment; values approaching 0 indicate
rapid, potentially disengaged responding. In PISA, RTE has been widely used
as a proxy for test-taking engagement and as a covariate in predictive
models of achievement and non-cognitive outcomes. Several studies in our
corpus use RTE as either a predictor (in ML models of achievement) or as an
outcome variable (in models of disengagement), and the rubric classifies
such uses under the Process-Data \& Engagement category of our taxonomy
(see \R{sec:taxonomy}).''}
\end{authorresponse}
